Tesla has started producing its Optimus humanoid at its Fremont, California factory, in a space once used to assemble electric vehicles. The company announced the move in its third-quarter shareholder letter. "The bots coming off this line are the first production-quality bots that we have manufactured," the letter says. "While we will continue to iterate on the hardware and manufacturing process, we have proven that the current manufacturing process for Optimus is suitable for a commercial product."

Elon Musk called it "early production" on the earnings call. The ramp will be slow and "somewhat bumpy because it is such a radically new product," he said. He still claimed Tesla will be able to build 20,000 of them per week by the end of 2027.

## What actually left the line

The public got no specifications, no price and no name for a first customer. The letter establishes only that a production area was built on the former car floor and that it turned out machines the company considers sellable. How many there are, what they do and where they will work remains undisclosed.

Musk himself listed what is not ready: the design still needs tweaks, along with safety and reliability, the service life — a robot cannot wear out in a month — and the AI, which is not yet good enough for open-ended tasks. The line is running not because the product is finished, but because the company chose to debug manufacturing in parallel with development.

## The quarter in numbers

Tesla's revenue was $28.04 billion against $28.09 billion a year earlier. Electric vehicle sales stayed at about $20 billion, with 486,532 deliveries — roughly 6,000 more than the previous quarter but fewer than in the same period last year. Revenue from solar and energy storage fell 16 percent to $2.8 billion; the company blamed tariff-related customer refunds. The services business grew 33 percent to $4.6 billion and delivered a record $734 million in gross profit.

That was not enough to cover spending on AI, self-driving and Optimus. Net income came in at $787 million against $1.37 billion a year earlier, a 43 percent drop. Lower regulatory credit sales also weighed on the result.

## Why 20,000 a week is a promise, not a forecast

On the same call Tesla pushed back the unsupervised version of its driver-assistance software again. In April Musk said the version without a driver would "probably" arrive in the fourth quarter of this year. This time Ashok Elluswamy, vice president of AI software, pointed to the second quarter of next year, when the first customer cars could join the robotaxi fleet. That is not an isolated slip but a pattern: Musk's product timelines regularly move to the right, and the 20,000-robots-a-week claim deserves the same discount.

Hitting 20,000 units a week means building about a million robots a year. No site in the world is close to that volume today, including Agility's RoboFab, designed for 10,000 machines a year. Even if Tesla adds a second line, it will need suppliers of actuators, gearboxes, batteries and sensors that do not yet exist at that scale.

## What to check independently

First, whether there is an outside customer rather than internal pilots at Tesla's own plants. Second, the price: without it, there is no way to calculate whether the robot pays back compared with a human or a fixed automation cell. Third, safety certification: Agility spent two years getting clearance to work next to people, and Tesla has not yet confirmed that bar. Fourth, mean time between failures. A production line ships hardware, but service life is judged in thousands of hours on site, not in a quarterly letter.

For now, one thing is clear: Tesla has a humanoid production line, and that alone is a shift for the industry. But profit fell 43 percent and the timelines for its other products moved again. Optimus now has to prove not that it can walk, but that it can work a shift and make money.
