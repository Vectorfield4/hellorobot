Audi confirmed at NVIDIA GTC Berlin that it is testing mobile humanoids at its Ingolstadt plant. The robots are meant to take parts from logistics bins and lay them out in the right order, the way a person does in kitting. The project runs with Agile Robots, Dassault Systèmes and NVIDIA and is still a proof of concept.

The target is not the assembly line but internal logistics. To feed the lines in the correct sequence, the plant performs about a million picking operations a day. The bins hold parts of different sizes, shapes and packaging, some of them soft. Conventional automation struggles with that mix: a fixed robot expects a repeatable part position.

## Who stands at the bin

The work is done by the German Agile ONE: about 1.74 m tall, 69 kg, two arms with a combined payload of up to 20 kg. The platform comes with legs or on a mobile base. Perception is cameras and lidar; grasping relies on tactile sensors in the hands. It moves at roughly an operator's speed, which matters: the robot has to fit aisles designed for people.

Human size is a condition of the job, not styling. Rebuilding a plant's logistics around a machine costs more than putting the machine into an existing environment.

## Digital twin first

Before letting a robot into the hall, Audi, Dassault Systèmes and NVIDIA built a digital twin of the kitting area: DELMIA on the 3DEXPERIENCE platform with NVIDIA Omniverse simulation libraries. The virtual scene is used to test scenario feasibility, cycle times, ergonomics and safety. The LEO virtual assistant, according to the partners, shortens some planning phases from months to days.

The same twin is where the humanoids learn to pick from mixed bins. Validated scenarios then move to the real hall: the robots perform kitting both in the virtual environment and on the Ingolstadt floor.

Three humanoids are involved today. Testing began in spring 2025 and was extended in summer 2025; Audi gives no end date and no deployment volumes.

## Why kitting is a sensible first station

The task combines regularity and variability. The regularity is that a part must be picked and placed, all day long. The variability is in the mixed bins and in parts that are soft, shiny or wrapped in film. For training this is easier than welding or assembly: mistakes cost less and data is plentiful.

The downside is arithmetic. A million operations a day and three robots in a pilot mean that even at full load the machines cover a fraction of a percent of the work. This is not shift replacement yet; it is a test of whether a humanoid survives an eight-hour cycle.

## What to check

First, cycle time and grasp success on mixed bins — that is where claims of generality break. Second, downtime for maintenance: a mobile humanoid has more wearing parts than a fixed arm, and each of them ages. Third, safety next to people in an aisle with moving equipment.

Audi came to the topic later than BMW and Hyundai, which already work with startups. Its stated goal is more modest than most press releases: not to replace workers but to find out what a humanoid actually gives car production.
