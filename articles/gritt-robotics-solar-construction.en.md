California-based Gritt Robotics says its systems are already working at solar sites run by contractor Burns & McDonnell. Overall the company is working with three top-10 solar engineering, procurement and construction firms and expects to install six million panels with them over two years — about 2.8 GW of capacity.

Gritt's approach differs from competitors that build specialised machines from scratch. It takes off-the-shelf construction equipment and adds computer control with AI. The logic is plain: Caterpillar and Bobcat know how to build reliable vehicles, FANUC, KUKA and Kawasaki know how to build arms, and there is no point reinventing the chassis.

> "Caterpillar and Bobcat know how to build good vehicles. Kawasaki, FANUC and KUKA know how to build good robots. Why should we reinvent the base platforms, which in many cases have been working in the harshest environments? We focus on writing the right brain to control them safely and repetitively."
>
> — Puneet Puri, co-founder and CEO of Gritt Robotics

## Why construction, and why solar

Robots work well in labs and factories where the variables are known. Outdoors there are too many: open trenches, people in the work area, snow and dust on materials, changing light. That is why construction sites remain manual.

Solar sites are a useful exception. They are large but regular: rows of posts, trackers and panels repeat, and the ground is surveyed. At the same time the job stays outdoors, with rain, mud and moving equipment.

> "Outdoors is hard. Outdoors, there is going to be a lot of variability. Our thesis is that solar offers an example of a construction site where there are a lot of physically demanding, repetitive steps and materials transport that can be automated."
>
> — Puneet Puri

Gritt builds an intelligence layer that understands site context: where a trench is open, where people are moving, what condition the materials are in. That layer can then be trained for a specific operation, from hauling and placing bases to fastening and running cable.

## The workforce argument

Industry reports cited by the publication put the shortfall of workers needed for current US solar projects at 53,000. About 41% of construction workers nationwide are expected to retire by 2031. Contractors already have to move crews to remote sites and house them.

So demand comes less from a wish to automate everything and more from an inability to fill a shift. Data centre construction and other infrastructure face a similar squeeze, and that is where Gritt intends to take what it learns on solar sites.

## What to check

First, which operations the machines actually perform today. Six million panels sounds impressive, but installing a panel has several stages, and automating some of them produces very different results. Second, how many people remain on site next to the robot: if the crew does not shrink, the economics change.

Third, the cost of the retrofit. Gritt sells not a robot but a control kit for existing equipment, and a contractor needs to compare the price per hour with an operator's. The company publishes no numbers.

Founders Puri and Dugar are graduates of Carnegie Mellon's Robotics Institute, which explains the bet on reusing platforms: the team has experience taking research systems into the field. How well that carries from snow and dust to a contractor's books will show next construction season.
