About 300 Boston Dynamics employees have signed a letter asking management to stop supplying Spot robots to US Immigration and Customs Enforcement. The Boston Globe reported the letter, which it reviewed, on 23 October.

The deal runs through a reseller: the robots come from Radeco, a Connecticut company, not directly from Boston Dynamics. The $1.3 million contract covers four machines in a configuration Radeco calls Cerberus, after the three-headed dog: a robotic arm, a thermal camera and sensors for explosive materials.

The letter was sent in September. Employees say they are "uncomfortable" with the deal and warn it could hurt the company's reputation and its workforce.

> "We cannot be proud of working at BD when that work is being used by a government agency that brutalizes civilians."
>
> — from the employees' letter

## The company's position

A Boston Dynamics spokesperson confirmed the letter reached senior executives. The company said the robot's purpose is to keep people safe, and that the Department of Homeland Security must follow its terms and conditions, which prohibit weaponisation and any use for harm or intimidation.

The same spokesperson told the paper that half of the roughly 300 signers did not use their names and that they represent a minority of the company's 1,400 workers.

Former interim chief executive Amanda McMaster answered the petition by email: she stands behind the decision to move forward with this and other potential sales to government customers. McMaster cited the company's anti-weaponisation guidelines and promised to make sure the machines are not misused.

> "We believe this decision is in line with our purpose of ethically advancing intelligent machines in service of humanity. Please accept this decision, even if you do not agree with it."
>
> — Amanda McMaster

The Department of Homeland Security said the robot gives officers an extra tool to assess a dangerous situation before people are put at risk.

## Why it matters beyond one contract

$1.3 million is small for the industry, but the case shows how the argument about "do not weaponise" versus "do not use" is structured. Formally Boston Dynamics does not sell the robot to the agency directly and does not mount a weapon on it. In practice the company takes part in technical support, and employees write that the arrangement presents them as "working directly with ICE".

Terms of use remain the only control mechanism once a machine is handed over, and compliance is hard for a manufacturer to verify. That is the point opponents make: activists from the De-Ice Action Network have staged protests outside Boston Dynamics' Waltham headquarters.

## What is still unknown

The company has not said which functions are included in the delivery or who will service the robots in the field. There is no data on how many machines have shipped. The letter establishes one thing: part of the staff believes the reputational cost is higher than the revenue, and some are ready to leave.

For the defence robotics market this is the first public case where internal dissent became part of the contract story instead of staying in internal email. How other manufacturers react will show over the next year: government demand is growing, and so are questions about where each company draws its line.
